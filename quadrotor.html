Embedded Control · CS/ME 410 · Northwestern

AUTONOMOUS
QUADROTOR CONTROL

"Our code was right. Our data was clean. And then it had to fly."

Goal

Build a quadrotor from bare parts and write every layer of its flight software — raw IMU registers to camera-based autonomous position hold.

Role

Controls & embedded software; two-person team with Michael Bayer.

Skills

C++ Python Cascaded PID control Complementary filtering BMI088 IMU / I²C Raspberry Pi Zero 2W ArUco camera tracking Shared memory

Outcome

Manual stabilized flight and camera-based X/Y/Z + yaw autonomy working; reached genuine autonomous flight, not fully fine-tuned before the 10-week quarter ended.

Ten weeks from a bare Raspberry Pi and an IMU to a quadrotor that holds its own position from an overhead camera. Michael and I built every layer by hand — sensor fusion, PID loops for pitch, roll, and yaw, and camera feedback on top. Here's the build, told through the data.

Sensing before controlling

You can't hold an angle you can't measure. A complementary filter fuses the noisy accelerometer with the drifting gyro into one clean signal — roll_filtered = ALPHA·roll_angle + (1−ALPHA)·(gyro_roll_dt + roll_filtered), with ALPHA = 0.02 (98% gyro).

P — the restoring force

The harder the frame tilts, the harder it torques back: thrust ± PITCH_P_GAIN·(pitch_desired − filtered_pitch), split across the four motors. I tuned the gain from 3 up to 6. Pure P chases every move — but with no damping, it overshoots and rings.

D — the damper (and a noise problem)

Derivative control brakes against rotation speed (the Y-axis gyro). But at ALPHA = 0.02 the estimate is 98% gyro, so any real D gain fed sensor noise straight through as high-frequency motor jitter. I pulled D back and pushed P higher instead.

P and D together — tracking

With P restoring and a modest D damping it, the loop finally tracked commanded pitch steps cleanly — no ringing, just a slight offset still left to fix.

I — closing the last gap

Integral control accumulates error over time to erase that leftover offset — clamped to ±I_SATURATE against windup. Combined as thrust ± P ± D ± I, the pitch axis became fast, damped, and dead-on.

Aside — tuning the sensor's own filter

The BMI088's own low-pass filter (acc_con, 0x40) swept across three bandwidths — 0x08 / 0x09 / 0x0A. Wider filtering smoothed the raw accelerometer; the fused estimate barely moved.

Accelerometer pitch (cyan) at IMU filter settings 0x89 / 0x99 / 0xA9, left to right — cleaner as bandwidth narrows.

From one axis to four

One tuned axis, copied to all four. I rebuilt motor mixing around three sign arrays (pitch_signs {−1,1,−1,1}, roll_signs {−1,−1,1,1}, yaw_signs {−1,1,1,−1}) so each motor is one clean expression, then added yaw as a rate P controller (YAW_P_GAIN = 5, 45°/s).

Handing control to a camera

An overhead camera tracked an ArUco tag and closed the outer loop. Yaw ran as two nested P loops; X/Y as PD controllers driving desired pitch and roll from camera position, blended 50/50 with the joystick. Tuning those gains was the fight — until we landed on P = 70, D = 15.

What the data couldn't show me

On screen, everything worked. In the air, a battery a millimeter off drifted it and a shift in lighting threw off the camera — every factor suddenly coupled to every other. We reached genuine autonomous flight across all three axes before the quarter ended, never perfectly tuned. The lesson: nothing behaves the way it does on the computer, and closing that gap is the whole job.
